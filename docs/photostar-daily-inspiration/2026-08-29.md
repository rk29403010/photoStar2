## ★ Best next idea — Relightable Evidence

Some historical surfaces are difficult because the information is physical relief rather than colour or tone: worn gravestones, memorial plaques, embossed photographs, handwriting impressed into paper, seals, coins, medals, stamped studio mounts, scratched captions, and shallow surface damage.

For these, ordinary contrast/sharpening is often the wrong tool. The strongest technique is often **multi-light imaging / Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI)**: photograph the object repeatedly from the same viewpoint while moving a light source around it, then reconstruct an interactively relightable surface.

Historic England explicitly documents low-cost H-RTI as a technique for revealing surface detail in cultural heritage. Hidden Heritage specifically calls out worn gravestones as a useful application.

Sources:
- Historic England multi-light imaging guidance: https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/multi-light-imaging-heritage-applications/
- Hidden Heritage digital imaging/gravestone RTI: https://hiddenheritage.org.uk/resources/digital-imaging/

### Why this fits PhotoStar

PhotoStar could treat a multi-light capture as one historical object with several source images plus a derived **relightable view**. The viewer would let the user drag a virtual light around the surface and choose enhancement modes without rewriting pixels or hallucinating letters.

This is particularly attractive for forensic restoration because it preserves a direct evidential relationship to the captures: the apparent illumination changes, but the underlying surface evidence does not.

**Effort:** Medium for an external-tool prototype; medium-high for integrated capture/processing.  
**Value:** High for inscriptions and textured artefacts; niche for ordinary family snapshots.

## Modern open-source implementation — Relight + OpenLIME

The old CHI RTIBuilder is now obsolete on modern operating systems. A National Gallery of Art technical evaluation notes that CNR-ISTI's newer **Relight** builder and **OpenLIME** browser viewer provide the modern route.

Official code:
- Relight: https://github.com/cnr-isti-vclab/relight
- OpenLIME: https://github.com/cnr-isti-vclab/openlime
- OpenLIME API/docs: https://cnr-isti-vclab.github.io/openlime/

Relight includes a GUI/CLI for processing image stacks, light-direction handling, normal-map extraction, RTI output and web publishing. OpenLIME is an ES6/WebGL JavaScript viewer that supports RTI, multispectral and layered imagery, annotations, measurements and multi-resolution image formats.

The key integration point is unusually good: PhotoStar is already React/Vite, while OpenLIME is a browser-side JavaScript/WebGL library rather than a monolithic desktop application.

A first prototype should therefore avoid reimplementing RTI mathematics. Generate a small RTI externally with Relight, embed OpenLIME in a PhotoStar experimental viewer, and test the UX on real difficult surfaces.

## UX — Read the Stone

A specialised evidence viewer could provide:

- **Original** — ordinary photograph.
- **Relight** — drag the virtual light around the surface.
- **Raking presets** — left/right/top/bottom grazing light.
- **Normals / surface enhancement** — optional analytical view.
- **Transcription** — structured names, dates, epitaph and notes.
- **Evidence marks** — annotate exactly where each interpreted character came from.

The critical rule is that a transcription should distinguish:

- clearly visible characters;
- probable interpretation;
- inferred/missing characters;
- unreadable text.

That is much more defensible than running a generative restoration and displaying a clean-looking inscription as if it were original evidence.

## Current genealogy product signal — transcription is still mostly human

Find a Grave currently places uploaded headstone photos into a transcription queue and supports multiple people plus inscriptions. BillionGraves similarly runs first- and second-pass human transcription and stores fields such as names, birth/death dates, marriage dates, military information and epitaphs. BillionGraves' mobile capture also attaches GPS so the stone can be mapped and revisited.

Sources:
- Find a Grave transcription: https://support.findagrave.com/hc/en-us/articles/53933320820371-Transcribe-Basics
- BillionGraves first-pass guide: https://support.billiongraves.com/support/solutions/articles/35000231420-transcription-guide-first-pass
- BillionGraves GPS records: https://support.billiongraves.com/support/solutions/articles/35000308535-what-are-billiongraves-headstone-records-what-is-the-difference-between-billiongraves-headstone-rec

PhotoStar's opportunity is not to become another public cemetery database. It is to bring the same structured evidence into a private family archive while giving the user substantially better tools for reading difficult inscriptions.

## OCR should be an assistant, not the authority

PaddleOCR is worth benchmarking as the generic recognition layer. The official project released **PaddleOCR 3.7 / PP-OCRv6 in June 2026**, with small models, broad Latin-script support and scene-text recognition. It also now has an official browser SDK for earlier PP-OCR models.

Official code/demo:
- https://github.com/PaddlePaddle/PaddleOCR
- The project links an official online Experience Center from its README.

But generic OCR is trained primarily on printed/scene text, not badly eroded carved lettering. PhotoStar should therefore compare OCR suggestions against the relightable evidence and expose confidence/alternatives rather than silently accepting text.

A useful workflow would be:

`multi-light captures → RTI/relight → choose best virtual-light views → OCR several views → consensus candidates → human transcription`

This uses AI where it saves work without confusing inference with observation.

## Research caution — do not turn damaged lettering into plausible fiction

Google DeepMind's **Aeneas** is a particularly useful conceptual reference. It contextualises and restores damaged Latin inscriptions and was evaluated in collaboration with historians; the Nature paper reports the strongest results when historians and the model worked together rather than either working alone.

Official resources:
- Public demo: https://predictingthepast.com/
- Official code/models: https://github.com/google-deepmind/predictingthepast
- Nature paper: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09292-5

Aeneas is for ancient Latin epigraphy, not modern gravestones, so it is not a PhotoStar dependency. The important product lesson is the separation between **what survives on the object** and **a scholarly/model restoration of what may have been there**.

For PhotoStar, inferred inscription text should always be visually different from observed characters and traceable back to the source region.

## Surface evidence as a reusable PhotoStar concept

The more interesting architectural step is broader than gravestones.

A new derived-result family such as `surface_evidence_v1` could describe:

- multi-light source group;
- alignment/capture geometry;
- RTI/normal-map derivative;
- annotated regions;
- observed transcription;
- alternative readings;
- provenance/confidence.

Consumers could include:

- gravestones and plaques;
- embossed/stamped studio mounts;
- blind-stamped photographer marks;
- handwriting impressions on album pages;
- coins/medals;
- scratched annotations;
- signatures and seals.

That makes this a small forensic-imaging capability rather than a cemetery-specific feature.

## Creative concept — The inscription wakes up

A presentation view could start with the ordinary stone exactly as photographed. As the user moves the pointer or tilts a phone, virtual raking light slowly sweeps across it and letters emerge naturally from the relief.

When a readable region becomes clear, a subtle transcription can appear beside it — never overpainting the stone.

For a family-story view this could move from:

**the grave as it looks today** → **the surviving inscription** → **the people/dates linked to PhotoStar** → **family photographs of that person**.

The transition is grounded in real captured surface geometry rather than a generated reconstruction. That could be visually striking while remaining unusually honest about the evidence.

## Best next experiment

Do not build RTI processing into PhotoStar yet.

Take **3–5 genuinely difficult textured subjects**, ideally including a worn headstone or plaque. Capture roughly 30–50 fixed-camera photographs while moving one light source around the object. Process them with the official Relight tools, then embed one result with OpenLIME in a throwaway React page.

Test three questions:

1. Can previously ambiguous lettering actually be resolved by interactive relighting?
2. Is the OpenLIME viewer responsive and pleasant enough to feel native inside PhotoStar?
3. Does OCR improve materially when run against selected raking-light renders rather than the original photograph?

If the answer to #1 is yes, I would add **Relightable Evidence** to the PhotoStar idea backlog even if full phone-guided capture comes much later.

The broader principle is strong: **when information is encoded in the physical surface, change the light before changing the pixels.**
