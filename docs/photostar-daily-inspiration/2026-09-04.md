## Best next idea — Photographic Process Passport

PhotoStar should model *what kind of physical photographic object a scan represents*, not only what the image depicts.

Examples include:

- albumen print
- silver-gelatin POP/DOP
- chromogenic colour print
- instant print
- tintype / ambrotype / daguerreotype
- postcard or carte-de-visite style mount
- photomechanical halftone / offset reproduction
- modern inkjet reprint
- unknown photographic process

The passport should remain evidence-based:

> **Probable process: chromogenic colour print**  
> Confidence: moderate  
> Evidence: resin-coated paper appearance; dye fading pattern; border geometry; estimated manufacture era.  
> Alternative: later inkjet copy of an older photograph.  
> Needs confirmation: reverse scan / edge detail.

The point is not museum-grade conservation diagnosis from one JPEG. It is to add an important evidence dimension that can constrain dating, preservation, restoration and provenance.

### User value

1. **Better dating.** A physical process gives date boundaries independent of clothing or faces.
2. **Safer restoration.** Different processes fail differently; colour dye fading, silver mirroring, print-screen texture and paper staining should not be treated as one generic defect.
3. **Copy detection.** A 1930 image represented by a modern inkjet copy is historically different from an original 1930 print.
4. **Better capture guidance.** PhotoStar can ask for the back, edges or oblique-light detail only when those views would resolve ambiguity.
5. **Richer storytelling.** “This is a hand-coloured albumen carte-de-visite” gives context about how the photograph was made and circulated.

**Implementation effort: medium for a useful first version; high for conservation-grade automatic identification.**

## Why this materially develops Date Detective

Earlier dating work concentrated on visual temporal clues such as people, objects, places and family chronology. Process Passport adds a different evidence class: the *material technology of the photograph*.

A date solver should eventually combine:

- depicted-world evidence — cars, clothes, hairstyles, buildings;
- family evidence — ages, births/deaths, events, co-occurrence;
- sequence evidence — album/roll/order;
- **object evidence — photographic process, mount, border, paper and reproduction method.**

These clues should remain independently inspectable rather than collapse into one unexplained year estimate.

## Strong external reference — Graphics Atlas

The Image Permanence Institute at Rochester Institute of Technology maintains **Graphics Atlas**, an object-based identification resource used by conservators and archivists. It covers dozens of photographic, photomechanical, printmaking and digital processes and provides high-magnification reference imagery and identification guidance.

Official interactive resource: https://new.graphicsatlas.org/explore

IPI overview: https://www.rit.edu/ipi/photographic-print-preservation

This is a much better domain reference than asking a general vision model to invent labels. It also suggests the UI pattern PhotoStar should steal conceptually: *compare observable characteristics, narrow possibilities, retain alternatives*.

## Current product signal — date estimation is becoming commodity

MyHeritage PhotoDater estimates dates for historical photographs from visual-era clues and reports a range/confidence; MyHeritage says roughly 60% of its test estimates fall within five years. It is proprietary and server-side.

Official current help page: https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/12852507-what-is-photodater-and-how-does-it-work

Timeline Scan is a newer service aimed at automatically dating large batches of scans and writing corrected dates back into metadata. Phototology similarly advertises evidence-chain dating using multiple visible anchors including fashion, vehicles, architecture and film-stock characteristics.

- https://timelinescan.com/
- https://phototology.com/families

PhotoStar's opportunity is not merely to produce another estimated year. It can retain *which independent evidence classes support or contradict that year*.

## Research/model experiment — do not trust zero-shot dating

The 2023 paper **Blind Dates: Examining the Expression of Temporality in Historical Photographs** tested OpenCLIP on almost 40,000 historical press photographs. Zero-shot dating was relatively weak and biased; fine-tuning improved results, and temporal objects such as cars and people proved informative.

Paper: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06633

I could not locate a clearly maintained author-provided runnable repository or hosted demo for this specific paper. Third-party pages claim code/notebooks, but that is not enough to treat them as official.

That result is relevant to Process Passport: do not assume a generic vision-language model will reliably distinguish subtle photographic processes zero-shot.

## Model Lab candidates

### SigLIP 2 — cheap zero-shot baseline

Google's SigLIP 2 is an official multilingual vision-language encoder with zero-shot classification and image-retrieval support. Official models are on Hugging Face and usable locally through Transformers.

Official model: https://huggingface.co/google/siglip2-base-patch16-256

Official usage/docs: https://huggingface.co/blog/siglip2

There is no official PhotoStar-like photographic-process demo. Use it only as a baseline against labels such as `albumen photograph`, `silver gelatin photograph`, `chromogenic print`, `halftone printed reproduction`, then measure error on known references.

### DINOv2 — exemplar retrieval rather than forced classification

Meta's DINOv2 is a strong self-supervised visual feature extractor with official code and pretrained models.

Official code/models: https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov2

No task-specific photographic-process demo exists. A potentially better experiment than classification is nearest-neighbour retrieval: compare an unknown scan against a labelled reference bank derived from legitimate process examples, then show the closest exemplars to the user.

That supports an explainable UI:

> Looks most like these 5 known chromogenic prints.

rather than:

> AI says chromogenic: 94%.

## Practical UI — Process Workbench

Add a small `Process` section to the photo/object inspector:

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Object type | photographic print |
| Probable process | chromogenic |
| Date compatibility | broadly compatible with 1960s–1990s |
| Original/copy status | uncertain |
| Evidence needed | reverse scan; edge/corner close-up |
| Confidence | moderate |

A button **Help identify this print** can open a guided checklist derived from observable traits rather than technical jargon first:

- Is the image on paper, metal or glass?
- Is the surface glossy, matte or metallic?
- Are there visible dots under magnification?
- Is colour in the image continuous or visibly applied?
- Is there manufacturer/studio printing on the reverse?

PhotoStar can hide impossible branches as evidence accumulates.

## Capture integration — Ask for the right extra view

This materially extends Capture Doctor.

Instead of asking users to scan every reverse and edge, PhotoStar should request extra evidence selectively:

> **Process uncertain — one extra view would help.**  
> Photograph the lower-right corner at an angle in side light.

or:

> **Possible postcard reproduction.**  
> Scan the reverse; publisher marks may date the object precisely.

Low effort for the user, high information gain.

## Restoration integration — Process-aware defaults

Do not let process classification silently alter pixels. Use it to choose analysis and warnings.

Examples:

- probable halftone → prioritise existing descreen tooling;
- chromogenic print → inspect channel-specific fading before generic colourisation;
- silver-gelatin print → look for silver-mirroring-like edge artefacts rather than treating them as ordinary glare;
- hand-coloured monochrome → preserve applied colour as historical evidence rather than 'correcting' it to neutral monochrome.

This makes Selective Restoration more historically aware without making it more generative.

## Creative concept — The Photograph Exploded

For selected objects, provide a visual storytelling mode that separates:

1. **the depicted moment** — who/what is shown;
2. **the photographic object** — paper, process, mount, handwritten reverse;
3. **the technology** — how that process worked;
4. **the historical context** — why families used that format at that time;
5. **the digital lineage** — scan → correction → restoration.

A family portrait stops being only 'Mum, 1964'. It also becomes:

> a small chromogenic print, probably commercially processed, carried in an album for decades, later scanned in 2026.

RIT's recent **Darkroom to Data** work is a useful artistic/research reference: it explicitly studies the material history of photography at scale and connects materials and processes with preservation, artistic intention and historical practice.

Project: https://www.darkroomtodata.org/

## Experimental context feature — Visual Echoes

Once process, date and visual embeddings exist, PhotoStar could optionally find *public historical images or art that echo a family photograph* without pretending they are evidence about the family.

For example, a 1930s family kitchen photograph could surface visually/temporally related open-access museum or archive material under a clearly separate **Context / Visual Echoes** heading.

Useful sources include Europeana's 60M+ cultural-heritage records and APIs, and Smithsonian Open Access.

- Europeana APIs: https://api.europeana.eu/en
- Smithsonian Open Access developer tools: https://www.si.edu/openaccess/devtools

The Cleveland Museum of Art's **Share Your View** is a small but elegant design reference: upload an image and receive visually similar collection works. It is not family-history software, but it demonstrates that visual analogy can make collection exploration playful rather than search-box driven.

Live tool: https://www.clevelandart.org/digital-innovations/share-your-view

The rule for PhotoStar should be strict: an external visual echo is *context/inspiration*, never evidence for identity/date/place unless an independent provenance relationship exists.

## Best next experiment

Build no UI first.

Create a labelled test set of about **150–300 images** spanning perhaps 8–12 broad classes:

- silver gelatin
- albumen
- chromogenic
- instant
- tintype/metal
- postcard/photomechanical
- halftone/offset reproduction
- digital/inkjet copy
- unknown/ambiguous

Use trusted museum/Graphics Atlas examples plus a small set of real family scans with known physical originals.

Compare:

1. SigLIP 2 zero-shot classification;
2. DINOv2 nearest-neighbour retrieval;
3. simple image features + user-entered physical clues;
4. combinations of 2 + 3.

Measure top-1/top-3 accuracy and, more importantly, whether the system reliably knows when it should say **unknown**.

My expectation: pure AI-from-a-flat-scan will be too brittle for trustworthy identification, while *retrieval + physical-object questions* could be genuinely useful with modest engineering effort.
