## Best next idea — Living Knowledge Rescue

PhotoStar already has multiple systems that *discover uncertainty*: unknown faces, fuzzy dates, unresolved places, ambiguous album groups, unlinked GEDCOM people and evidence conflicts. The next step should not be another recognition model. It should help recover facts that still exist only in living relatives' memories.

The core feature is a **Knowledge Gap Queue**. PhotoStar ranks questions by expected information gain and by how many archive items an answer could resolve.

Example:

> **Ask Mum: Who is the woman beside Jean?**  
> Appears in 17 photographs, 1958–1966. Resolving her identity may also settle 3 album groups and 2 GEDCOM candidates.

or:

> **Ask Peter: Was this the Coltishall house or the earlier Norwich one?**  
> Answer would locate 26 photographs and narrow their date range.

This materially develops the earlier Memory Session / Family Chorus and Contribution Inbox / Question Packs ideas: instead of generic prompts, PhotoStar generates questions directly from unresolved archive evidence and records exactly what each answer changes.

**Effort:** medium for the first useful version.  
**Value:** very high, because some evidence is perishable in a way that files are not.

## Why this direction is stronger than generic memoir software

Current products validate the low-friction voice workflow. Remento centres on voice answers, photos and automatic story conversion; Storyworth now offers phone recording, guided interviews and family calls; VoiceHistory stores audio with searchable transcripts; Permanent.org emphasises preservation, collaboration and FamilySearch integration.

The opportunity for PhotoStar is different: **the archive decides what to ask next**.

- Storyworth: https://welcome.storyworth.com/voice
- VoiceHistory: https://voicehistory.com/
- Permanent.org: https://www.permanent.org/

## UI — Questions worth asking

A single low-effort dashboard:

| Priority | Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High | Who is this woman? | 17 photos + 2 GEDCOM candidates |
| High | Was this before or after the move? | narrows 41 photo dates |
| Medium | Is this Skegness or Mablethorpe? | locates one Memory Bundle |
| Low | Whose dog was this? | affects 3 photos only |

The ranking should favour:

1. questions that resolve many assets;
2. questions with a small candidate set;
3. contradictions that a human can settle quickly;
4. evidence attached to an elderly or uniquely knowledgeable witness;
5. questions that are easy to answer from one shown photograph.

Do **not** show a depressing “memories at risk” score. Just put the best five questions at the top.

## Photo-prompted interviews

Professional oral-history guidance explicitly recommends research before interviewing, open-ended prompts, follow-up questions, preservation of the original recording, time-coded access aids, and keeping related photographs/documents connected to the interview. Photo-elicitation is also an established technique: showing photographs can trigger richer recollection than abstract questioning.

PhotoStar is unusually well placed to automate the preparation stage because it already has the source material.

A session could therefore start with 6–10 high-value photographs rather than a generic life-history questionnaire.

Example flow:

1. show one photograph;
2. ask one targeted but open question;
3. record the answer;
4. ask a follow-up only when the answer introduces a useful unresolved person/place/event;
5. move on before the session becomes work.

Reference guidance:
- Oral History Association Best Practices: https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/
- Oral History Society — family historians: https://ohs.org.uk/for-family-historians/

## Technical experiment — speaker-attributed, word-timed evidence

### WhisperX

WhisperX adds word-level forced alignment and can combine transcription with speaker diarization. The maintained official implementation provides code and documents demos.

Official code: https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX

For PhotoStar the important output is not polished prose. It is:

```text
00:03:12.420–00:03:15.880  ROBIN: Who is standing beside Jean?
00:03:16.240–00:03:22.610  MUM: That's Auntie Margaret. She lived in Worksop then.
```

Now a claim can point to the exact original words that support it.

### pyannote.audio

pyannote's current `community-1` speaker-diarization pipeline runs locally and is specifically designed to make speaker assignment easier to reconcile with transcription timestamps.

Official code: https://github.com/pyannote/pyannote-audio  
Official model: https://huggingface.co/pyannote/speaker-diarization-community-1

There is no need to use the premium cloud service for the initial benchmark.

## Data model — answer ≠ fact

An interview should never silently overwrite archive metadata.

Store something like:

```text
MemoryClaim
  speaker: Mum
  recording: interview-2026-09-05.wav
  start: 196.24
  end: 202.61
  claim: person(face_883) = Margaret Smith
  certainty: speaker-says-confidently
  status: proposed / corroborated / disputed
```

That drops neatly into Claims & Evidence.

If two relatives disagree, retain both recollections.

## Better than automatic memoir writing — Question Chaining

The AI use I would prioritise is not “rewrite this into a touching story”. It is detecting high-value follow-ups.

Suppose the answer is:

> “That's Margaret. She lived in Worksop then, before she married Bill.”

PhotoStar can notice three useful new assertions:

- identity = Margaret;
- place association = Worksop;
- event ordering = before marriage to Bill.

Then it can choose **one** follow-up:

> “Do you remember roughly when she married Bill?”

The aim is maximum archive information for minimum interview fatigue.

## Preserve the voice, derive the story

Remento and Storyworth both demonstrate demand for turning recordings into readable narrative. PhotoStar can do this too, but the hierarchy should be:

1. original audio — primary recollection;
2. verbatim transcript;
3. extracted claims and links;
4. edited readable transcript;
5. optional narrative/story presentation.

Never make the polished narrative the only surviving representation.

That also follows oral-history archival guidance, which stresses preservation of originals plus contextual and rights metadata.

## Creative concept — The Photograph Talks Back

Open an old photograph years later.

Instead of an AI-generated avatar, a small waveform appears beside a person:

> **Mum remembered this photograph in 2026 — 1m 14s**

Tap it and hear the real voice while the photograph remains still.

As the recording plays, unobtrusive links appear when mentioned:

- “Margaret” → that person's archive thread;
- “Worksop” → Place Thread;
- “before she married Bill” → Time Fog / family event.

This is emotionally richer than synthetic animation precisely because nothing is fabricated.

## Experimental concept — Memory Coverage Map

Once several interviews exist, PhotoStar could show *where living testimony exists and where it does not*.

Not as a generic completion percentage. Show the archive itself:

- people with first-hand recollections;
- events with only second-hand stories;
- photographs nobody has discussed;
- claims independently remembered by several people;
- unresolved areas where only one likely witness remains.

That would turn oral history into part of archive provenance rather than a folder of WAV files.

## What I would test next

Use 100–200 real photographs with known metadata and deliberately hide selected identities/places/dates.

Build only a read-only prototype that:

1. finds unresolved high-impact clusters;
2. generates five candidate questions;
3. ranks them by how many uncertainties an answer could resolve;
4. records a short conversation;
5. runs WhisperX + pyannote locally;
6. lets a human attach one transcript span to one proposed claim.

The success measure is not transcription accuracy alone. It is:

> **Did a 10-minute conversation permanently resolve useful parts of the archive with less effort than manual cataloguing?**

If yes, Living Knowledge Rescue deserves to become a first-class PhotoStar workflow.

The core principle is:

> **Files can be rescanned later. Knowledge that exists only in one person's memory may not be recoverable later, so PhotoStar should know what is worth asking while the answer still exists.**
