## Best next idea — Handwriting Threads / Who wrote this?

PhotoStar should treat handwriting itself as an identity-bearing visual signal.

A family archive contains writing in places that are easy to overlook:

- backs of photographs;
- album captions;
- postcards;
- letters and diaries;
- labels and envelopes;
- notes written decades after the photo was taken.

Once a few writing samples are confidently attributed to a person, PhotoStar could offer:

> **Probably written by Margaret**  
> Similar hand appears on 23 other items.  
> Strongest evidence: capital `M`, long descenders, digit `7`, spacing pattern.

The point is not forensic certainty. It is candidate retrieval: **find me everything that appears to have been written by the same hand**.

That would connect naturally to existing concepts including Memory Bundles, Claims & Evidence, Date Detective, Album Archaeology and Living Knowledge Rescue.

**Effort:** Medium for useful retrieval; high for robust automatic attribution.  
**Potential value:** Very high on real family archives.

## Why this is worth separating from transcription

Current genealogy/document products increasingly focus on reading handwriting. Transkribus now has a genealogy-specific workflow and public upload demo, with hundreds of pretrained handwriting models and entity tagging. MyHeritage launched Scribe AI in March 2026 for transcription, translation and interpretation of letters, photographs and other historical images. LifeArchive similarly makes handwritten journals and letters searchable.

Those products validate **handwriting-to-text**.

PhotoStar can add a different question:

> **Whose handwriting is this?**

The text and the writer are separate evidence streams. Two postcards saying the same thing can have completely different provenance; two unrelated captions can become connected because the same person wrote both.

## New 2026 research signal — writer retrieval at scale

ICDAR 2026 includes **Writer Retrieval at Scale** by Raven, Hallyburton and Fink. The work introduces FormWR: almost 400,000 pages associated with nearly 100,000 writer proxies, including sparse handwriting, printed form clutter and pages containing multiple hands. Their X-VLAD retrieval approach reports 97.9% top-1 accuracy and 78.8% mAP on that benchmark, and transfers to historical handwriting fragments.

Official author code:

https://github.com/Traven16/writer-retrieval-at-scale

This is substantially closer to PhotoStar's likely problem than ordinary handwriting classification because PhotoStar usually wants:

> **find similar writing elsewhere in my own archive**

rather than choosing from a fixed list of 100,000 known writers.

I found official code, but no author-hosted simple upload-and-test demo.

## Another highly relevant 2026 benchmark — handwriting changes over a lifetime

**BullingerDB: A Dataset for Handwritten Text Recognition and Writer Retrieval** contains 20,898 pages, roughly 499,000 text lines, 796 writers and six decades of writing. The authors specifically evaluate writer retrieval over time and report that temporal variation makes the problem materially harder.

Paper:

https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30235

Dataset resources are available through the IAPR TC-11 historical document repository. I could not find a clearly identified official code repository or hosted inference demo for the 2026 BullingerDB writer-retrieval experiments, so I would not imply one exists.

The PhotoStar lesson is important: **a person's handwriting changes through life**, just as their face does.

So eventually this wants the handwriting equivalent of Lifetime Identity Anchors:

- Margaret, teenage letters;
- Margaret, 1950s album captions;
- Margaret, later-life cards.

Do not collapse all of them into one average style if age-aware retrieval works better.

## Practical first feature — Same Hand

Put one action on any selected handwritten region:

> **Find the same handwriting**

Return visually similar writing snippets across the archive.

Examples:

- caption on photo back → another caption;
- album annotation → postcard;
- envelope → letter;
- recipe card → later note.

Initially PhotoStar does **not** need to know the writer's name.

It can simply create:

> **Handwriting cluster 12 — 31 items**

Then the user identifies one trusted sample and the cluster becomes a candidate identity source.

That feels much safer than training a closed-set classifier on tiny family samples.

## Model Lab route

### 1. Current writer-retrieval research

Benchmark the official `writer-retrieval-at-scale` code if its model/checkpoint setup is practical enough.

### 2. Older Vision Transformer baseline

Koepf, Kleber and Sablatnig's **Writer Identification and Writer Retrieval Using Vision Transformer** has official reproducibility code and pretrained-result infrastructure:

https://github.com/michaelkoepf/writer-identification-and-writer-retrieval-using-vision-transformer

The repository was archived in February 2025, so it should be treated as a research baseline rather than a dependency.

### 3. Generic visual embeddings

Also test DINOv2/SigLIP-style generic embeddings on cropped handwriting. It may turn out that a specialist model only modestly improves retrieval on a small family archive.

The benchmark question should be simple:

> given one known handwriting sample, does the correct writer appear in the top 5 results?

## Handwriting recognition — useful, but keep it modular

For local handwriting transcription, **Kraken** remains an attractive open-source candidate. It is explicitly optimised for historical/non-Latin material, supports trainable layout analysis and character recognition, outputs PAGE XML/ALTO/hOCR, and has a public model repository.

Official code:

https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken

Kraken does not provide the same zero-setup family-history experience as Transkribus; it is infrastructure. That actually suits PhotoStar's Model Lab philosophy.

Transkribus remains the stronger immediately testable hosted benchmark:

https://www.transkribus.org/genealogy

It provides a live family-document upload demo. For privacy/local-first PhotoStar, I would use it as an accuracy comparison rather than assume it becomes the production backend.

## Data model — handwriting attribution must be a claim

Suggested shape:

```text
HandwritingSample
  source_asset
  region
  transcript
  transcript_confidence
  writer_candidate
  attribution_status
  visual_embedding
  date_range
```

And crucially:

> `writer_candidate = Margaret` is a claim supported by handwriting similarity, not a fact created by the model.

Human-confirmed samples can become anchors for later retrieval.

## Useful UI — Writer Strip

Once a writer is identified, PhotoStar could show a chronological strip of their handwriting:

> **Margaret's hand**
>
> 1948 letter → 1956 album caption → 1964 postcard → 1989 birthday card

That may help resolve uncertain attributions visually, and it is also a genuinely human way to browse family history.

A person's handwriting is a surviving physical trace of them, not merely text waiting to be OCR'd.

## Creative concept — Notes in Their Hand

When viewing a person's page, optionally show tiny real handwriting fragments beside relevant events:

> **1958 — Skegness**  
> _“lovely weather again”_ — scanned from postcard, Margaret's probable hand

Clicking the fragment reveals the full source object and attribution evidence.

Do not generate imitation handwriting. Use only genuine scanned fragments.

This creates a strong visual distinction between:

- typed metadata written now;
- words actually written by the family member then.

## Navigation benefit for large archives

Handwriting clusters create another useful way through a chaotic collection.

Instead of browsing 4,000 unidentified backs and notes, PhotoStar can say:

> **5 recurring hands detected**
>
> Hand A — 83 items  
> Hand B — 41 items  
> Hand C — 19 items

Identifying one cluster might unlock names, places and date clues across dozens of photographs at once.

That makes it another form of **semantic compression**, like Memory Bundles and Identity Solver.

## What I would test next

Use perhaps **100–200 known handwriting samples** from 5–10 people, deliberately spanning different years and source types.

1. Crop handwriting regions manually for the benchmark.
2. Try generic DINOv2/SigLIP-style embeddings.
3. Compare against one specialist writer-retrieval implementation.
4. Measure top-1 and top-5 retrieval by writer.
5. Split the results by time gap: same decade vs decades apart.
6. Separately compare Kraken and Transkribus on transcription accuracy; do not mix transcription quality with writer retrieval.

If a single handwriting sample can reliably pull back several other items by the same person, **Same Hand** is worth building before any bespoke writer classifier.

The central principle is:

> **Handwriting is not only text to be read. In a family archive, the hand itself is evidence about who created the object.**
