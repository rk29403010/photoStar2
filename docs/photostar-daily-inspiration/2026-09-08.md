# PhotoStar2 Daily Inspiration — 8 September 2026 — Wardrobe Threads

## Repo signal

`main` still has no application-code commits after 8 August; the 26 August commit remains documentation-only. The current face pipeline already persists face embeddings and person assignments in `derived_results` / `face_assignments`, while PhotoStar also already has segmentation/model infrastructure. A repository search found no explicit clothing/outfit identity layer.

That creates a useful unexplored evidence source: **what people are wearing**.

## Best next idea — Wardrobe Threads / Event Stitcher

PhotoStar should be able to answer:

> **Find this same coat/dress/jumper again.**

Not merely “find photos containing a red dress”. The stronger archival question is whether two photographs show the **same physical garment or outfit**.

That can help when:

- a face is blurred, turned away or unidentified;
- scans from one event have become separated into different folders;
- several rolls/albums cover the same wedding, holiday or Christmas;
- an approximate date is uncertain;
- visually similar relatives need extra context;
- a sequence has no usable timestamps.

The output should remain evidence, not certainty:

> **Probable same outfit** — blue patterned dress + white cardigan; 11 photographs across two scan folders.

A single garment match is weak evidence. Matching several garments, people, place and photographic characteristics becomes much stronger.

**Effort: Medium. Value: High, with an unusually cheap first experiment.**

## Why current photo software leaves room for this

Current products increasingly make clothing *searchable* without treating garments as persistent archive entities.

- Immich's current contextual search uses CLIP-style vector search and can combine it with people, albums and other filters.
- Mylio's local AI search combines SmartTags, OCR, facial recognition and metadata for object/scene queries.
- MyHeritage PhotoDater explicitly uses clothing, hairstyles, furniture and other period cues when estimating historical dates; its July 2026 documentation says about 60% of its test photographs landed within five years of the true date.

Those validate clothing as useful visual information. PhotoStar's opportunity is different: **instance-level recurrence** rather than just category or decade recognition.

## Practical first version — Same Outfit

From a person/photo inspector:

> **Find same outfit**

PhotoStar crops the visible clothing regions and retrieves similar clothing crops elsewhere in the archive.

Results might show:

| Result | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Photo 2184 | probable same dress + cardigan |
| Photo 9912 | same jacket, different trousers |
| Photo 10331 | visually similar coat, probably different |

User actions:

**Same outfit · Shares garment · Different · Unsure**

Those few confirmations create durable positive/negative examples without forcing manual tagging.

## Technical path — use segmentation PhotoStar already wants

Do not begin with fashion taxonomy.

A minimal pipeline could be:

1. use an existing person/body segmentation result to isolate torso/legs or clothing-like regions;
2. create an embedding for each crop;
3. nearest-neighbour search within plausible date/person/event ranges;
4. combine independent region matches into an outfit-level score.

The key is to avoid letting faces dominate the embedding. A crop focused on the garment is much more useful than embedding the entire person photograph.

## Model Lab candidate — Marqo FashionSigLIP / FashionCLIP

Marqo's open fashion-specific CLIP/SigLIP models are trained for fine-grained fashion retrieval and classification. The official repository provides weights, Hugging Face models and an **official Hugging Face Space demo**. The models can also be loaded with Transformers/OpenCLIP, and the model card documents Transformers.js usage, which is interesting for PhotoStar's JS/TS stack.

Official code: https://github.com/marqo-ai/marqo-FashionCLIP

Official model: https://huggingface.co/Marqo/marqo-fashionCLIP

This should be treated as a benchmark candidate, not assumed to work on faded 1940s family prints: its domain is modern fashion/product imagery.

## Research reference — DeepFashion2

DeepFashion2 is useful because it explicitly benchmarks **clothing-instance retrieval** across viewpoint, scale, occlusion and consumer/shop domains. It contains hundreds of thousands of clothing images with masks, landmarks and matched clothing identities.

Official dataset/code: https://github.com/switchablenorms/DeepFashion2

There is no simple author-hosted upload-and-test demo; the official repository provides the dataset/evaluation resources.

Again, PhotoStar's domain is harder in different ways: monochrome scans, ageing prints, partial bodies and decades-old clothing. The benchmark nevertheless demonstrates that “same clothing item across different photographs” is a tractable computer-vision task distinct from generic image similarity.

## Research warning — clothing is useful precisely because it is *not* identity

Clothes-changing person-ReID research exists because ordinary person-recognition systems can over-rely on clothing. The CVPR 2025 **DIFFER** project explicitly disentangles biometric cues from clothing, pose and hairstyle.

Official author code: https://github.com/xliangp/DIFFER

I found official runnable code/checkpoints but no simple author-hosted interactive upload demo.

That suggests a strong PhotoStar data-model rule:

> **person identity and outfit identity must remain separate signals.**

A coat can connect photographs from one event; it must never become evidence that two faces are the same person across years.

## Material development of Date Detective — Outfit Date Constraints

MyHeritage shows that fashion can inform broad historical dating. PhotoStar can do something more archive-specific.

Suppose a dress appears in:

- one photograph securely dated June 1968;
- another securely dated September 1968;
- four undated photographs with the same wearer and garment.

The undated photos gain a **family-specific date clue** that may be substantially stronger than generic fashion-era recognition.

Store it as:

> same garment as dated photos — supports 1968-ish event/date range

not:

> AI date = 1968.

This fits Time Fog / Claims & Evidence cleanly.

## Event Stitcher

The strongest user-facing payoff may not be clothing search at all.

When PhotoStar sees:

- same three people;
- same outfits;
- similar place/background;
- scans scattered across folders;

it can propose:

> **These 23 photographs may belong to one event.**

That helps reconstruct events even when scan order and EXIF are useless.

Clothing becomes one weighted edge alongside faces, place, date, print/film characteristics and album/roll evidence.

**Effort: Medium after garment embeddings. Value: Very high for disordered inherited scans.**

## UI idea — Outfit Strip

On a person's page, optionally show outfits as compact clusters rather than individual photos:

`[blue dress] 11 photos` · `[dark suit] 7 photos` · `[striped jumper] 5 photos`

Clicking an outfit opens the corresponding event/neighbourhood in the normal gallery.

This reduces dozens of near-related images into human-recognisable visual handles without introducing another global gallery layout.

## Creative concept — Family Wardrobe Timeline

A restrained storytelling view could show **real cropped garment fragments** across decades:

> 1948 school cardigan → 1956 wedding suit → 1964 holiday dress → 1981 winter coat

The point is not fashion history for its own sake. It creates a tangible timeline of a person's life using objects that physically travelled with them.

External historical context can then be optional and clearly separated:

> similar period clothing in museum/catalogue collections

while the family garment itself remains primary evidence.

## Experimental concept — Hand-me-down detection

A more speculative extension is detecting the **same physical garment appearing on different people at different times**.

That could reveal genuine family stories:

> probable same child's coat appears on Jean in 1954 and Margaret in 1957.

This should require human confirmation because generic uniforms, school clothes and mass-produced garments can look nearly identical. But when confirmed, it creates an Object Thread with unusual emotional and historical value.

## What to test next

Take roughly **200 photographs from 5–10 known events**, deliberately including repeated outfits and visually similar but different clothes.

Benchmark:

1. whole-person generic embeddings;
2. segmented-clothing generic embeddings;
3. Marqo FashionCLIP/FashionSigLIP embeddings;
4. optionally simple colour/texture features as a cheap baseline.

Measure:

> **Given one garment/outfit crop, is another photograph from the same event/outfit in the top five?**

Then test whether adding the clothing score improves event grouping beyond faces/date/place alone.

If it does, the best next implementation is **Event Stitcher powered by garment recurrence**, not a standalone fashion browser.

The core principle is:

> **What someone wore is weak evidence about who they are, but often strong evidence about which photographs belong to the same moment.**
